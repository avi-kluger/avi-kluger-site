1. Teaching Listening: Classroom-Ready Exercises

Type
Workshop (based on the AOM 2026 PDW)
Length
90–180 minutes
Audience
Management and business faculty; doctoral students
Developed with
N. Meikle (Kansas), K. Emich (Delaware), E. Campbell (Minnesota)

Listening is increasingly recognized as a strategic leadership competency, yet it is rarely treated as a trainable skill. This workshop first gives participants the joyful experience of being listened to. It then equips them with classroom-ready, evidence-based exercises for teaching listening, exercises that move beyond hard-to-master “active listening.” Each exercise is debriefed against the principles of Episodic Listening Theory.

Six exercises participants practice

  • Accepting offers (drawn from playback theatre).
  • Inviting stories to initiate listening episodes.
  • Time sharing.
  • Asking destructive questions.
  • Asking constructive questions.
  • Listening circle.

Attendees leave with a clear rationale for giving listening curricular attention, hands-on experience with scalable exercises, and access to online resources (exercise instructions, debrief prompts, and teaching adaptations) for immediate use in business-school courses.

Anchored in

Lehmann, M., Kluger, A. N., Cojuharenco, I., & Itzchakov, G. (2026). Cultivating humility in business education: A listening-focused pedagogy for future leaders. Journal of Business Ethics, 204(1), 145–162. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10551-025-06099-2

2. The Power of Listening at Work

Type
Research talk / keynote
Length
45–90 minutes
Audience
Faculty, doctoral students, general academic

A research keynote on why listening matters and how it works. Episodic Listening Theory holds that high-quality listening sets off a virtuous dyadic cycle: the listener enhances the speaker’s authenticity, which in turn deepens listening, culminating in rare moments of togetherness. In these moments, people tolerate paradox, think creatively, and leave with greater clarity, heightened well-being, and stronger attachment.

The talk pairs theory with evidence. Meta-analyses show that the strongest correlates of perceived listening are relationship-quality variables (e.g., trust), followed by positive emotions, flexible cognition, and superior performance; experiments support some of these effects. In the domain of attitudes, high-quality listening makes them more complex and less extreme.

Anchored in

Kluger, A. N., & Itzchakov, G. (2022). The power of listening at work. Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior, 9, 121–146. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-orgpsych-012420-091013. 3. The Social Relations Model: Studying Dyadic Behavior

Type
Research / methods talk
Length
60–90 minutes
Audience
Faculty, doctoral students, quantitative methodologists

People behave differently in the company of specific others in ways that cannot be predicted from either person’s personality. These unique dyadic combinations explain a sizeable amount of variance in behavior. This talk introduces the Social Relations Model (SRM) as a conceptual and methodological tool for studying dyadic behavior and demonstrates its use in management and beyond.

It reviews the designs in which SRM applies: round-robin, asymmetric-block (e.g., speed-dating), one-with-many reciprocal, and the half-block (e.g., committee members rating multiple candidates). It shows, for example, that the dyad explained over 40% of the variance in listening within work teams, that even trait-like humility is modulated dyadically, and that dyadic variance can be estimated for binary outcomes.

Kluger, A. N., Malloy, T. E., Pery, S., Itzchakov, G., Castro, D. R., Lipetz, L., Sela, Y., Turjeman-Levi, Y., Lehmann, M., New, M., & Borut, L. (2021). Dyadic listening in teams: Social relations model. Applied Psychology: An International Review, 70(3), 1045–1099. https://doi.org/10.1111/apps.12263

Lehmann, M., Pery, S., Kluger, A. N., Hekman, D. R., Owens, B. P., & Malloy, T. E. (2023). Relationship-specific (dyadic) humility: How your humility predicts my psychological safety and performance. Journal of Applied Psychology, 108(5), 809–825. https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0001059

4. Why Won’t You Listen?

Type
Faculty-development workshop
Length
60–90 minutes
Audience
Lecturers and faculty across the social sciences

If listening produces such desirable outcomes, why do people so often fail to do it well? This talk, with workshop elements, first grounds participants in the theory and evidence for listening’s benefits. It then turns to the obstacles, which are the heart of the session.

Obstacles examined

  • Cognitive demand: listening well is effortful.
  • Avoidance-attachment style.
  • Status concerns.
  • Second-hand (vicarious) trauma.
  • Resistance to change.

Each obstacle is paired with fledgling research supporting it and with practical implications for teaching, supervising, and leading. The session suits lecturers from all faculties of the social sciences.

5. The Feedforward Interview

Type
Executive / applied workshop (flagship)
Length
90 minutes to full day
Audience
HR and OB faculty interested in feedback; executives, managers, and executive education

The Feedforward Interview (FFI) is a listening-based alternative to the traditional performance appraisal. Rather than evaluating past shortcomings, it elicits a concrete story of a peak experience and mines it for the conditions that bring out a person’s best. A routine conversation thereby becomes a developmental, relationship-building encounter. The approach has been featured in the Financial Times and the Chicago Tribune.

Participants receive

  • A structured FFI protocol they can apply immediately with their teams.
  • A take-home conversation booklet.

Anchored in

Rechter, E., Kluger, A. N., & Nir, D. (2025). The feedforward interview: A theoretical account. Human Resource Management Review, 35(2), 101061. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hrmr.2024.101061

Budworth, M.-H., Latham, G. P., & Manroop, L. (2015). Looking forward to performance improvement: A field test of the feedforward interview for performance management. Human Resource Management, 54(1), 45–54. https://doi.org/10.1002/hrm.21618

Budworth, M.-H., & Latham, G. P. (2025). The feedforward interview: An innovative approach to performance appraisal. Organizational Dynamics, 54(1), 101089. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.orgdyn.2024.101089

6. Inner-Conflict Resolution and Well-Being

Type
Research talk
Length
60–90 minutes
Audience
Faculty and doctoral students in organizational behavior, social, clinical, and positive psychology
Developed with
Dina Nir (Ono Academic College)

Everyone knows the experience of being torn between two alternatives, and the evidence is consistent that such inner conflict lowers well-being. Are people doomed to suffer from it? This talk argues that they are not. The conflict itself is functional: it signals that the alternatives on the table leave some needs unmet, and it recruits resources to deliberate about how to satisfy them.

The talk integrates theories of the multifaceted self, research on negotiation, and work on ambivalence and paradox mindset into a single model. In that model, deliberation is a negotiation among parts of the self, and two constructs explain how it ends. Decision integration is the degree to which a chosen alternative satisfies the needs on both sides of the conflict. The decision-integration threshold is the personal benchmark above which a person stops deliberating and decides. The higher the integration of the chosen solution, the higher the well-being that follows.

Searching for integrative alternatives and holding a high threshold both consume resources. When resources are scarce, or when a person treats the conflict as either/or, the threshold drops and the search halts. The model thus explains why deliberation so often ends in a decision that leaves needs unmet, and why the same conflict then returns.

Anchored in

Nir, D., & Kluger, A. N. (2026). Inner-conflict resolution and well-being [Manuscript under revision].

7. The Negotiating Self Method: Resolving Inner Conflicts

Type
Workshop
Length
90–180 minutes
Audience
Faculty and doctoral students; executives and managers; coaches and counselors
Developed with
Dina Nir (Ono Academic College)

Participants bring a real inner conflict of their own and resolve it with the Negotiating Self Method (Nir, 2016). The method is a structured protocol that treats deliberation as a negotiation among parts of the self and aims at a win-win decision. It lowers the cognitive effort of mapping the needs behind a conflict, and it replaces the tacit either/or frame with an explicit both/and one.

Four steps participants practice

  1. Frame the conflict as two concrete poles, and set the goal of a win-win resolution.
  2. Identify all internal perspectives: record every consideration for each pole in a two-column map, asking “What else is relevant?”
  3. Reveal underlying needs: the deeper hopes, aspirations, concerns, and fears beneath each pole.
  4. Build an integrative solution: ask of each need, “How can I honor this need in my decision?”, until no mapped need is left unaddressed.

The method works as written self-help, and theory predicts a stronger effect when a listener administers it. Participants therefore work in pairs, once as the speaker and once as the listener, which ties the method to the listening research above. They leave with the protocol and with a worked map of their own conflict.

Nir, D., & Kluger, A. N. (2026). Inner-conflict resolution and well-being [Manuscript under revision].
